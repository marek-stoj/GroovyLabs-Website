Groovy Labs Audio-Visual Ideas.md

Question

I have this idea. What if we could take sensor data - headset, controllers, hands, full body estimation etc. and somehow map it to frequencies and then to WaveBinder/AudioLink. This could let people change visuals with their movement, right? But how to map movement to frequencies? 🤔

Answer

Yes. That is a very good idea.

What you are describing is basically turning the player into a live modulation source. Not just “music drives visuals”, but “music + body drives visuals”. The player becomes part instrument, part spellcaster, part walking signal processor 🎛️🕺

And yes, the main trick is this:

do not literally map movement directly to audio frequencies at first. Map movement to control signals, then optionally project those signals into frequency bands.

Because “frequency” in audio-land is a weird beast. A hand moving left is not naturally “63 Hz”. If you force it too early, the system becomes arbitrary and hard to author.

Why not map movement straight to frequencies?

Because frequencies imply oscillation over time.

A raw position is not a frequency. A velocity spike is not a frequency. A pose is not a frequency.

A frequency means something repeating with some periodicity.

So you have two different kinds of mapping:

1. Feature-to-band weighting

You do semantic routing:

  • strong downward slash -> boost low band
  • lateral sweep -> boost mids
  • finger jitter / sparkle motion -> boost highs
  • crouch rise -> sub swell
  • torso twist -> mid resonance

This is not “real frequency analysis” of the body. It is expressive mapping, which is often better.

2. Motion-as-signal analysis

You treat movement over time as a waveform and analyze it.

Example:

  • take right hand Y position over time
  • store last 1-2 seconds
  • run smoothing
  • optionally run FFT / autocorrelation / zero-crossing / band-pass filters
  • estimate dominant oscillation rate

Then:

  • slow cyclical arm wave might become low-frequency modulation
  • fast wrist shaking might become high-frequency modulation

This is closer to true motion-frequency mapping.

The easiest practical mapping strategies

A. Band by body region

Very authorable.

  • head -> ambient / mids
  • left hand -> lows
  • right hand -> highs
  • hips / torso -> low-mid body pulse
  • feet / step cadence -> kick-like low pulse

This is simple and intuitive.

B. Band by motion type

Much better.

  • large slow motion -> low frequencies
  • medium rhythmic motion -> mids
  • tiny fast motion -> highs

Example:

  • both arms sweeping wide = low-end swell
  • torso rocking = mid texture
  • fingers / wrist flutter = bright shimmer

This feels natural because it mirrors how people already perceive movement scale.

C. Band by derived temporal frequency

Most “scientific”.

For each tracked signal:

  • sample over time
  • compute oscillation rate
  • map low oscillation rates to low visual bands
  • map faster oscillation rates to higher bands

Example:

  • 0.5-1.5 Hz body sway -> low band
  • 2-4 Hz arm rhythm -> mid band
  • 5-10 Hz jitter/shake -> high band

This is elegant, but noisier and trickier.

Best approach for Groovy Labs

I would use a hybrid:

Channel 1: physical intensity

Derived from:

  • hand velocity
  • controller acceleration
  • head acceleration
  • body COM velocity

This becomes:

  • player_energy

Channel 2: rhythmic cadence

Derived from:

  • repeated movement timing
  • stroke intervals
  • step timing
  • gesture repetition

This becomes:

  • player_rhythm

Channel 3: spatial expansion

Derived from:

  • hand distance
  • arm span
  • chest openness
  • body extension

This becomes:

  • player_spread

Channel 4: twist / rotation

Derived from:

  • torso yaw delta
  • shoulder differential
  • hand circularity

This becomes:

  • player_twist

Channel 5: verticality

Derived from:

  • crouch depth
  • hand elevation
  • head height delta

This becomes:

  • player_lift

Channel 6: micro-detail

Derived from:

  • small fast hand jitter
  • finger motion
  • controller gyro activity

This becomes:

  • player_spark

Then map those to pseudo-frequency bands:

  • low_mod = energy * 0.5 + spread * 0.3 + lift * 0.2
  • mid_mod = rhythm * 0.5 + twist * 0.3 + energy * 0.2
  • high_mod = spark * 0.6 + rhythm * 0.25 + lift * 0.15

Now you have band-like control without pretending the body is an MP3.

If you really want “movement to frequencies”

Here are the legitimate ways:

1. Oscillation rate estimation

For a tracked scalar signal like hand Y:

  • keep recent window
  • remove drift / DC offset
  • smooth
  • detect dominant periodicity
  • convert repetition rate to a band

Example:

  • hand bobbing at ~1 Hz -> low
  • wrist flutter at ~6 Hz -> high

2. Multi-scale filters

Instead of FFT, run several temporal filters:

  • very slow band: 0.2-1 Hz
  • slow band: 1-2 Hz
  • medium band: 2-4 Hz
  • fast band: 4-8 Hz
  • very fast band: 8-12 Hz

Feed movement signal into all of them. Whichever responds most strongly tells you where the motion “lives”.

That is often more robust than FFT in gameplay.

3. Gesture classifier -> synthetic band trigger

Recognize gestures and assign band responses.

  • punch -> low transient
  • sweep -> mid whoosh
  • flutter -> high sparkle
  • jump / body rise -> low bloom pulse
  • spin -> wide-spectrum burst

This is not analysis, but it is expressive and stable.

What signals to sample?

Not raw transforms only. Use derived values.

Good inputs:

  • position delta
  • velocity magnitude
  • acceleration magnitude
  • angular velocity
  • jerk
  • curvature of trajectory
  • symmetry between left/right hands
  • distance between joints
  • pose openness
  • periodicity score
  • stillness score

Example: A hand position alone tells little. A hand velocity curve over time tells a story.

A practical mapping example

Say the user is dancing in a Groovy Labs environment.

Inputs:

  • left hand speed = 0.8
  • right hand speed = 1.1
  • torso twist = 0.6
  • hand distance = 0.9
  • head bob cadence = 2.1 Hz
  • micro-jitter = 0.2

Derived:

  • energy = 0.85
  • spread = 0.9
  • rhythm = 0.7
  • twist = 0.6
  • spark = 0.2

Mapped:

  • low_mod = strong
  • mid_mod = medium-strong
  • high_mod = weak

Visual result:

  • environment pulses deep and wide
  • tunnel expands with arm spread
  • geometry swirls with torso twist
  • less glitter because micro-jitter is low

That will feel much better than “left hand x-position = 220 Hz”.

The real design question

Ask this:

Do you want the body to influence visuals continuously, or perform them?

Those are different modes.

Mode A: expressive modulation

Movement continuously shapes visuals. Very fluid, ambient, dance-friendly.

Mode B: gesture triggers

Specific motions produce distinct visual events. More gamey, more readable.

Mode C: conductor mode

Movement reshapes how the song’s own reactive system behaves. This is the most interesting one.

For example:

  • music provides base band data
  • player motion multiplies or remixes those bands
  • player is not replacing the song, but sculpting it

That is probably the sweetest spot for Groovy Labs.

The strongest Groovy Labs version

Do this:

final_visual_band = audio_band * audio_weight + body_band * body_weight + gameplay_band * gameplay_weight

So:

  • the song keeps identity
  • the player adds expression
  • gameplay adds dramatic beats

That gives you:

  • reactive worlds
  • dance-driven stage control
  • social performance potential
  • user-generated “movement visualizers”

My concrete recommendation

For v1, do not start with true movement FFT.

Start with:

  • velocity magnitude
  • angular velocity
  • hand distance
  • crouch / height
  • rhythm from peak intervals
  • optional gesture tags

Then expose:

  • low / mid / high body modulation
  • pulse
  • spread
  • twist
  • sparkle

This gives you 80% of the magic with 20% of the pain.

Then later, for v2, add:

  • periodicity estimation
  • motion band-pass filters
  • gesture classifier
  • per-song mapping presets

Tiny rule-of-thumb mapping set

A very solid first pass:

  • low band

    • body sway
    • crouch-rise
    • wide arm motion
    • step impact

  • mid band

    • arm swings
    • torso rotation
    • hand travel arcs

  • high band

    • wrist flicks
    • finger motion
    • rapid controller angular changes

  • pulse

    • motion onsets / peaks in acceleration

  • ambient

    • head motion and stillness balance

Stillness is interesting too. When the player freezes, visuals can “focus” or crystallize. Silence in movement can be as expressive as motion.

Short answer to the “how to map to frequencies?” part

Three good answers:

  1. fake it semantically Map kinds of motion to low/mid/high bands.

  2. derive temporal frequency Analyze how quickly motion oscillates over time.

  3. use body expression channels first Then remap them into whatever WaveBinder/AudioLink expects.

Number 3 is the best product decision.
